# Technical Report: Numerical Solution of the One-Dimensional Infinite Potential Well

## Abstract

The one-dimensional infinite potential well is used as a benchmark for numerical quantum mechanics. Analytical energy levels are compared with energies obtained by discretising the time-independent Schrödinger equation using a central finite-difference approximation. The resulting symmetric tridiagonal Hamiltonian is diagonalised with SciPy. Wavefunctions, probability densities, normalisation, and grid convergence are also examined. For an electron in a box of length 1 nm, the analytical ground-state energy is approximately 0.376030 eV; the calculation with 200 intervals gives approximately 0.376022 eV.

## 1. Introduction

The infinite potential well illustrates energy quantisation and the role of boundary conditions in quantum mechanics. It is also a useful numerical benchmark because an exact analytical solution is available. The numerical output can therefore be compared with a known reference.

The aims are to calculate analytical energy levels, solve a discretised Schrödinger equation, compare numerical and analytical energies, visualise wavefunctions and probability densities, and investigate grid convergence.

## 2. Theory

The potential is

\[
V(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&0<x<L,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Inside the well, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is

\[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi.
\]

The infinite walls impose \(\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0\). The analytical solutions are

\[
E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots
\]

and

\[
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right).
\]

The probability density is \(P_n(x)=|\psi_n(x)|^2\), with normalisation

\[
\int_0^L|\psi_n(x)|^2\,dx=1.
\]

The \(n\)-th state has \(n-1\) internal nodes. Doubling \(L\) reduces every energy level by a factor of four because \(E_n\propto L^{-2}\).

## 3. Numerical method

The interval \([0,L]\) is divided into \(N\) equal intervals with spacing \(h=L/N\). The central finite-difference approximation is

\[
\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}\bigg|_{x_i}\approx
\frac{\psi_{i+1}-2\psi_i+\psi_{i-1}}{h^2}.
\]

Substitution into the Schrödinger equation gives

\[
-C\psi_{i-1}+2C\psi_i-C\psi_{i+1}=E\psi_i,\qquad
C=\frac{\hbar^2}{2mh^2}.
\]

This is represented as \(\mathbf H\boldsymbol{\psi}=E\boldsymbol{\psi}\). The Hamiltonian is symmetric and tridiagonal, with \(2C\) on its main diagonal and \(-C\) on its neighbouring diagonals. The boundary values are fixed to zero; therefore, \(N-1\) interior points are unknown.

For the main calculation, the electron mass is \(9.1093837139\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\), the reduced Planck constant is \(1.054571817\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), the box length is \(1.0\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(N=200\). The Hamiltonian is consequently \(199\times199\).

The matrix is diagonalised using SciPy's `eigh_tridiagonal` routine. Energies calculated in joules are converted to electronvolts using \(1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\).

The analytical ground-state energy is approximately

### 4.2 Grid convergence

The convergence analysis calculates the ground-state energy for multiple values of \(N\). Refining the grid reduces \(h\) and generally brings the numerical energy closer to the analytical value. The leading error of the central finite-difference approximation is second order in grid spacing; in the asymptotic regime, the energy error scales approximately as \(h^2\), or \(1/N^2\) at fixed \(L\).

Use the plots generated by your local run to present the actual convergence results.

### 4.3 Wavefunctions and probability densities

The analytical wavefunctions are sinusoidal. The first state has no internal nodes, the second has one, and the \(n\)-th state has \(n-1\) internal nodes. Probability densities are non-negative because they are given by \(|\psi|^2\).

For this uniform-grid infinite-well problem, the discrete eigenvectors have the same sampled sine structure as the analytical solutions. Once normalised and aligned in sign, the sampled wavefunctions can agree to floating-point precision even though the finite-difference energies differ from the continuum energies. This is a special property of this problem, not a general expectation for arbitrary potentials.

### 4.4 Normalisation

The continuous normalisation condition is \(\int_0^L|\psi(x)|^2dx=1\). The numerical eigenvectors are normalised as discrete vectors; dividing by \(\sqrt h\) gives a continuum-scaled representation on the grid. Numerical integration of these wavefunctions gives a norm close to one.

## 5. Error analysis

The relative percentage error in an energy level is

\[
\epsilon_n(\%)=
\frac{|E_n^{\mathrm{num}}-E_n^{\mathrm{exact}}|}
{|E_n^{\mathrm{exact}}|}\times100.
\]

The error does not vanish at finite grid spacing. It decreases as the grid is refined. Energy error and wavefunction error must be assessed separately: a small energy error alone does not establish that every property of a numerical state is accurate.

## 6. Discussion

The project demonstrates how a differential eigenvalue equation can be converted into a matrix eigenvalue problem. The finite-difference Hamiltonian reproduces the expected ordering and approximate values of the energy levels. Grid-convergence analysis tests whether the calculated ground-state energy approaches the analytical benchmark as the grid is refined.

The model is intentionally simple. An infinite well has idealised boundaries and does not represent a realistic molecular potential. More advanced systems generally require additional approximations and more involved numerical methods.

## 7. Limitations

- The potential walls are infinitely high and the well is one-dimensional.
- The finite-difference method has discretisation error.
- The sampled wavefunction comparison is unusually favourable for this special system.
- Results depend on grid resolution and numerical implementation.
- This project is a foundational benchmark, not a molecular electronic-structure calculation.

## 8. Conclusion

The project implemented analytical and numerical treatments of the one-dimensional infinite potential well. A tridiagonal finite-difference Hamiltonian was diagonalised to obtain numerical energy levels and eigenvectors. Numerical energies agreed closely with the analytical benchmark, and convergence analysis demonstrated the importance of grid resolution. Wavefunction and probability-density analysis connected the matrix results to the physical interpretation of quantum states. This workflow provides a foundation for subsequent computational studies of more complex quantum systems.
